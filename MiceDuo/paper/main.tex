\begin{abstract}
A tabular foundation model (TFM) learns a stream through its context, so the choice of which past rows to condition
on is the learning algorithm. A natural choice is \emph{relevance selection}: at every step, rank recent labelled
batches by how well they agree with the newest batch and condition on the most relevant ones, not on the most recent.
We find that relevance selection performs very unevenly across streams. On seven development streams it matches the accuracy of
\mice{}, a mixture of in-context experts with a worst-case safeguard, but it loses up to half a point to \mice{} on
some streams and, used alone, up to 4.6 points to a plain window. We show that this variance is not a reason to drop
selection but a reason to treat it as an expert. Inserted as one more expert into \mice{}, unchanged, the selected
context inherits \mice{}'s any-time regret bound against the plain window, and empirically it costs at most about
0.15 points against \mice{} on any development stream while keeping most of its gain where concepts recur. On
eighteen fresh synthetic streams with controlled recurrence, a pre-registered test is consistent with a recurrence-based explanation: the gain
over \mice{} grows with how often concepts recur ($+0.28$, $+0.15$ and $+0.01$ points for concepts that appear five
times, three times and once), is largest for concepts that are hard to learn, and is never below $-0.01$ points on
any stream (rounded). A frozen evaluation on 22 held-out real-stream prefixes and three backbone seeds passes the
same no-negative-effect criterion on every seed: \method{} is at most 0.141 points below \mice{} or FIFO on
any stream and is $+0.05$ to $+0.08$ points above \mice{} on average, so its value on real streams is safety at a
small gain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN \citep{hollmann2025tabpfn} and TabICL \citep{qu2025tabicl} predict in one
forward pass given a context of labelled rows \citep{muller2022pfn,nagler2023statistical}. On a data stream they
replace incremental learners: the model is frozen and the context is updated \citep{lourenco2026streams}. What the
model knows about the current concept is then exactly the set of rows in its context, and the stream learner reduces
to a rule for choosing that set.

Two rules are in use. A \emph{window} conditions on the most recent rows; it is safe but forgets a concept as soon as
it leaves the window. A \emph{memory} keeps past segments as stored contexts and decides which to recall; \mice{}
\citep{mice2026} mixes windows and stored segments under a two-level meta expert and guarantees, with no assumption
on the stream, that it is never much worse than the window. Both rules choose contexts made of contiguous rows.

A third rule is suggested by the analysis of non-IID batches \citep{zhao2024wnb}: the error of a learner trained on
a set of batches decomposes into a term that grows with the discrepancy between those batches and the test batch,
and a term that shrinks with their size. For an in-context learner this is the trade-off between a large context and
a relevant one. \emph{Relevance selection} resolves it per step. It ranks the recent labelled batches by the loss of
the TFM conditioned on the newest batch, keeps the most relevant ones together with a short recent anchor, and
conditions on this non-contiguous context. We call the pair of this context and the window \duo{}.

\textbf{The problem.} Relevance selection is attractive and unreliable. On the seven development streams of
\cref{sec:exp-dev} \duo{} is as accurate as \mice{} on average, gains 1.5 points on a stream whose concepts recur,
and loses about half a point on two streams where they do not; the selected context alone is 4.6 points below a
plain window on one stream (\cref{tab:dev}). The tested repairs of the rule itself fail: a pre-registered search over the size
of the recent anchor traded the losses for the gains and passed on no seed (\cref{sec:exp-anchor}).

\textbf{Our answer.} We do not choose between selection and memory. We insert the selected context as one more
expert of \mice{}, with \mice{} unchanged. The consequence is immediate on the theory side and measurable on the
empirical side.
(i)~\mice{}'s slow level is a meta expert over two predictions, the window and the fast mixture; what the fast
mixture contains does not enter its bound. The any-time discounted regret of the combined learner against the window
is therefore at most $2\ln 2$, as for \mice{} (\cref{cor:safe}).
(ii)~\mice{}'s fast level reweights its experts every batch from their last loss, so a selected context that was clearly
worse on the last labelled batch loses most of its weight; the slow level bounds the cumulative cost otherwise.
(iii)~Against \mice{} itself there is no guarantee, and we measure it: on the development streams the combined
learner is at most about 0.15 points below \mice{} on any stream and seed and is above it on average
(\cref{tab:verdict}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{(1)}~Relevance selection of non-contiguous contexts for TFMs on streams, derived from the discrepancy--size
trade-off of non-IID batches, and the observation that its performance is very uneven across streams.
\textbf{(2)}~A plug-in construction that turns any context-selection rule into an expert of \mice{} with the
window safeguard intact.
\textbf{(3)}~Pre-registered evidence: on development streams the plug-in reduces the losses of selection to within a
pre-specified tolerance; on
eighteen fresh streams with controlled recurrence its gain over \mice{} grows with recurrence and with the difficulty
of the concept, and no stream loses more than about 0.01 points (\cref{sec:exp-mech}); on 22 held-out real-stream prefixes the
frozen criterion holds on all three backbone seeds (\cref{sec:exp-heldout}).

\section{Setting and Background}
\label{sec:setting}

\textbf{Protocol.} A stream arrives in batches $X_1,X_2,\dots$ of $B$ rows; the labels of batch $t-1$ are revealed
before batch $t$ is predicted. A frozen TFM $f_\Theta(\cdot\mid X;\cC)$ predicts class probabilities for the rows of
$X$ given a context $\cC$ of at most $M$ labelled rows. A stream learner chooses $\cC_t$ from the labelled rows of
batches $1,\dots,t-1$, or combines the predictions of several contexts.

\textbf{\mice{}.} \mice{} \citep{mice2026} keeps a set $\mathcal E$ of in-context experts: FIFO windows of 100, 300 and
$M$ rows, and up to 12 stored contexts of past segments. Its \emph{fast level} mixes all experts with weights
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fast}
  w_{t,k}\propto\exp\bigl(-\eta\,\ell^{\log}_{t-1,k}\bigr),\qquad k\in\mathcal E,
\end{equation}
where $\ell^{\log}_{t-1,k}$ is the log-loss of expert $k$ on the last labelled batch and $\eta=10$. Its \emph{slow
level} mixes the fast mixture $p^{\mathrm{fast}}_t$ with the $M$-row window $p^{\mathrm{win}}_t$ by exponential
weights on discounted half Brier scores $\ell_{t,k}\in[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slow}
  \begin{aligned}
  L_{t,k}&=\gamma L_{t-1,k}+\ell_{t,k},\qquad v_{t,k}\propto e^{-\eta_2\gamma L_{t-1,k}},\\
  p_t&=v_{t,0}\,p^{\mathrm{win}}_t+v_{t,1}\,p^{\mathrm{fast}}_t,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=0.9$ and $\eta_2=\tfrac12$. In the implementation both discounted losses start at zero, the outer loss
is $0.5$ times the half Brier score with unit step, and the weights use $\gamma L_{t-1,k}$ (tempered), which is the
same rule; all results in this paper use these settings.

\begin{theorem}[\citealp{mice2026}, any-time safeguard]\label{thm:outer}
For $K$ predictions combined by \eqref{eq:slow} with $\eta_2\le\tfrac12$, every sequence of labels and predictions,
every $T$ and every $k$:
$\sum_{t\le T}\gamma^{T-t}\bigl(\ell_t(p_t)-\ell_{t,k}\bigr)\le\ln K/\eta_2$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Relevance Selection}
\label{sec:duo}

\textbf{Motivation.} \citet{zhao2024wnb} bound the risk of a learner trained on a weighted set of non-IID batches by a
term in the discrepancy between each batch and the target distribution, weighted by the batch's share, plus a term
that decreases with the effective sample size. Small batches are individually far from the target; a large set of
them is close only if they are relevant. A TFM does not accept sample weights, so we implement the weights as a hard selection.

\textbf{Rule.} At step $t$ the candidates are the labelled batches $t-H,\dots,t-1$ with $H=100$. A recent anchor
$\mathcal A_t$ of the last $a$ batches is always kept. Every older candidate $b$ is scored by the mean log-loss of
the TFM conditioned on the anchor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score}
  s_t(b)=\tfrac1B\textstyle\sum_{(x,y)\in b}-\log f_\Theta(y\mid x;\mathcal A_t),
\end{equation}
a model-oriented discrepancy between batch $b$ and the current concept in the sense of \citet{zhao2023rdiv}. The
selected context $\cC^{\sel}_t$ is the anchor together with the $M/B-a$ lowest-scoring older batches; its prediction
is $p^{\sel}_t=f_\Theta(\cdot\mid X_t;\cC^{\sel}_t)$. All scores use labels of batches before $t$ only. Early
in a stream, when there are at most $M/B-a$ older candidates, all are kept without scoring.

\textbf{\duo{}.} The original proposal mixes $p^{\sel}_t$ with the $M$-row window by exponential weights on
discounted log-losses ($\eta=2$, $\gamma=0.5$) and uses $a=1$ (an anchor of one batch).

\textbf{Cost.} Scoring takes one TFM call with up to $(H-a)B$ query rows, evaluated in chunks; predicting takes one
call with $B$ query rows. On the held-out streams the selection expert adds 30\% to \mice{}'s logical TFM calls on
average (12\% to 43\%), but its scoring call is large, and the measured time per batch of \method{} is 1.9 times that
of \mice{} on average (1.2 to 3.9; \cref{sec:exp-heldout}).

\section{\method{}: Selection as an Expert}
\label{sec:method}

\method{} is \mice{} with one more expert: $\mathcal E'=\mathcal E\cup\{\sel\}$, where expert $\sel$ predicts
$p^{\sel}_t$. Nothing else changes: the fast weights \eqref{eq:fast} now range over $\mathcal E'$, and the slow level
\eqref{eq:slow} still combines the $M$-row window with the fast mixture. No constant is re-tuned.

\begin{corollary}[The safeguard survives any added expert]\label{cor:safe}
For every stream, every selection rule and every $T$, the discounted half-Brier regret of \method{} against the
$M$-row window, and against its own fast mixture, is at most $\ln 2/\eta_2=2\ln2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{thm:outer} holds for arbitrary predictions $p_{t,k}$. The slow level of \method{} has $K=2$ predictions, the
window and the fast mixture over $\mathcal E'$; which experts the fast mixture contains is not used.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is not guaranteed]
\Cref{cor:safe} compares \method{} with the window, not with \mice{}. Adding an expert changes the fast mixture, and
the fast mixture of \mice{} is not one of \method{}'s slow-level experts, so \cref{cor:safe} says nothing about the difference to \mice{}. One way to obtain an analogous guarantee
against \mice{} is to include its full output as a third slow-level expert; we do not evaluate this and instead keep
\mice{} unchanged and measure the difference (\cref{sec:exp-dev}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Down-weighting after an observed loss gap]
An expert whose log-loss on the last labelled batch exceeds the best by $\Delta$ has fast weight at most
$e^{-\eta\Delta}$ by \eqref{eq:fast}; with $\eta=10$, a selected context that was 0.3 nats worse on the last batch
keeps less than 5\% of the fast weight on the next. This says nothing about the next batch's loss under arbitrary
drift. When no expert is separated, the slow level decides whether following the fast mixture is worth
it, which is the case \cref{cor:safe} covers.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Horizon.} Relevance selection only sees the last $H$ batches; \mice{}'s stored contexts have no horizon. The
two are complementary: selection recovers concepts that recurred within $H$ batches at the granularity of single
batches, stored contexts recover older concepts at the granularity of segments.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\textbf{Protocol.} As \mice{}: $B=100$, $M=1000$, labels one batch late, TabPFN v2 with four estimators. Every
comparison is on the same batches with the same backbone seed. All decision rules were written and committed before
the corresponding runs; we report every pre-specified variant, including failures. The 22 real streams not used in
development are reserved for frozen confirmation. Backbone seeds change model randomness on fixed data and are not independent
data replicates.

\subsection{Development Streams: Selection Alone and as an Expert}
\label{sec:exp-dev}

Seven real streams (Elec2 and six streams used in \citet{mice2026}'s evaluation), first 300 batches, backbone seeds 1
and 2. \Cref{tab:dev} reports accuracy. The selected context alone is the least accurate learner on average, yet on Rialto, whose concepts recur, it is
6.32 points above FIFO and 1.24 above \mice{} (computed before rounding). \duo{} matches \mice{} on average but is 0.50 and 0.47 points below it
on Airlines and Weather. \method{} keeps the average and shrinks these losses to $-0.08$ and $-0.01$ points.

\subsection{The Tested Anchor Variants of Selection Alone Fail}
\label{sec:exp-anchor}

Before \method{} we tried to make \duo{} safe by itself, by enlarging the recent anchor to 300 and 500 rows so that the
selection only fills the rest of the context. The rule required, on every backbone seed, a mean gain over \mice{} of
at least 0.5 points, wins on at least 5 of 7 streams and no stream more than 0.3 points below FIFO. No variant passed
on any seed. Larger anchors moved \duo{} towards FIFO: they reduced the worst loss against FIFO (from about $-0.55$ to
$-0.19$ with anchor 300) and removed the gain on Rialto (from $+1.35$ to $-0.47$ against \mice{}); these figures average backbone seeds
0--2, whereas \cref{tab:dev} averages seeds 1--2 (Rialto: $+1.47$). Even an oracle that
picks the better of \duo{} and \mice{} per stream after the fact is only 0.23--0.28 points above \mice{}.
\Cref{tab:anchor} in \cref{app:anchor} lists every variant and seed.

\subsection{When Does Selection Help? Controlled Recurrence}
\label{sec:exp-mech}

Eighteen fresh synthetic streams from \mice{}'s grid generator, unused by either method: fifteen blocks of 2000 rows,
pure real drift (shared inputs, permuted centroid labels), with $K\in\{3,5,15\}$ distinct concepts so that each
concept appears 5 times, 3 times, or once; 30 or 100 centroids; three data seeds. Pre-registered hypotheses:
(H1) on every stream without recurrence, accuracy at most 0.30 points below \mice{} and at most 0.30 points below
FIFO; (H2) the same two bounds on every stream,
with mean log-loss at most 0.005 above \mice{}; (H3) the gain over \mice{}
increases with recurrence ($K=3>K=5>K=15$) and is positive on at least 5 of 6 streams at $K=3$. Before the results, in our run log rather than in the frozen plan, we
noted that with blocks of 20 batches and $H=100$, a concept with $K=5$ returns exactly at the edge of the selection
horizon, and predicted that the gain at $K=5$ might be close to that at $K=15$.

\textbf{Results.} \Cref{tab:mech} reports accuracy by recurrence and difficulty. All three hypotheses hold for the
primary variant. The mean gain over \mice{} is $+0.28$ points when each concept appears five times, $+0.15$ when it
appears three times and $+0.01$ when it never recurs; at $K=3$ it is positive on all six streams. No stream is more
than about 0.01 points below \mice{} ($-0.010$ at worst), every stream is above FIFO, and the mean log-loss is 0.0015 lower than \mice{}'s. Our
prediction for $K=5$ was wrong: its gain is about half of that at $K=3$, well above $K=15$, so the selection horizon
did not cancel the benefit of recurrence. The gain concentrates on hard concepts: with 100 centroids it is $+0.46$,
$+0.26$ and $+0.02$, with 30 centroids $+0.10$, $+0.04$ and $+0.01$. This matches the learning-curve account of
\citet{mice2026}: recalling rows pays when a concept takes many rows to relearn. The anchor-300 selection, reported as
a secondary variant, passes the same checks. The increment is small next to \mice{}'s own gain over FIFO on these
streams (6.3 to 14.5 points). We do not measure whether the selected batches belong to the recurring concept, so the
ordering is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, retrieval of matching concepts.

\subsection{Held-Out Real Streams}
\label{sec:exp-heldout}

Following the mechanism test, we froze a confirmation on 22 real-stream prefixes not used to develop
\method{}, although they were previously used to evaluate \mice{}. We use the first 300 complete batches
(or all complete batches for shorter streams), with batch 0 reserved for warm-up, and backbone seeds 0, 1 and 2.
The primary method and all constants remain unchanged. The decision requires, separately for each seed,
that every stream lose at most 0.30 accuracy points against both \mice{} and FIFO, that mean gain over
\mice{} be non-negative, and that mean log-loss be at most 0.005 above \mice{}.
We report every stream and seed, including failures. The streams form only four source groups
(Airlines, Covertype, Poker and INSECTS); source summaries average streams within source before averaging
the four sources. Neither the 22 streams nor the 66 stream--seed pairs are independent replications.
The evaluation also records the incremental selection cost.

\textbf{Results.} The frozen criterion holds on every seed (\cref{tab:heldout-verdict}). Over the 22 streams
\method{} is $+0.060$, $+0.078$ and $+0.048$ points above \mice{} on average for seeds 0, 1 and 2, no stream is more
than 0.134 points below \mice{} or 0.140 below FIFO, five streams per seed are below \mice{}, and the mean log-loss is
0.0008 to 0.0012 lower. By source (\cref{tab:heldout}) the gain is largest on Poker ($+0.15$) and positive on Airlines
and INSECTS ($+0.07$, $+0.06$); on Covertype \method{} is consistently but negligibly below \mice{} (between $-0.02$ and
$-0.01$ on every seed). Averaged over sources, \method{} is $+0.07$ points above \mice{} and $+0.50$ to $+0.59$ above FIFO. These
results confirm the development finding on unseen streams: selection as an expert does no measurable harm and adds a
small gain; they do not show a large gain on real streams.

\textbf{Cost.} The selection expert adds 30\% to \mice{}'s logical TFM calls on average (12\% to 43\% across
streams), with 571 fits and 2\,714 forward calls per stream on average. Its scoring call predicts up to $(H-a)B$ rows,
so time grows faster than calls: the measured time per batch of \method{} is 1.9 times that of \mice{} on average
(1.2 to 3.9). These times were measured with up to eight concurrent processes on one GPU and include contention; they
describe this run, not an isolated latency benchmark.

\textbf{Execution.} The run used one, then up to eight, then two or three concurrent workers because of
intermittent CUDA failures on our machine; each change was recorded as a dated amendment before any score was
inspected, the snapshot, tasks, data and evaluator were unchanged, and failed batches were recomputed from checkpoints
in new processes. Predictions are bit-identical across concurrency levels.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{In-context stream learning.} TFMs with sliding memories outperform adaptive tree ensembles on drifting
streams \citep{lourenco2026streams}, and drifting priors improve them under temporal shift \citep{helli2024drift}.
\mice{} \citep{mice2026} adds a memory of stored contexts with a worst-case safeguard. Relevance selection is a third
way to build a context, at the granularity of batches and within a horizon.

\textbf{Recurring drift.} Pools of models with reuse decisions \citep{goncalves2013rcd,gama2014recurrent} and the
mixture of online and offline experts \citep{zhao2025mooe} recall trained models; drift detectors reset them
\citep{gama2004ddm}. See \citet{gama2014survey,lu2019learning} for surveys.

\textbf{Discrepancy and batch weighting.} R-divergence measures model-oriented discrepancy \citep{zhao2023rdiv};
weighting non-IID batches by discrepancy improves out-of-distribution detection \citep{zhao2024wnb}. Our score
\eqref{eq:score} is a one-batch model-oriented discrepancy used for selection rather than weighting.

\textbf{Expert advice.} Exponential weights with exp-concave losses \citep{cesabianchi2006prediction,hazan2007log}
make adding an expert cheap in regret; \cref{cor:safe} is an instance at the level of contexts.

\section{Limitations}

The gain of \method{} over \mice{} is small: $+0.15$ points on the development streams, mostly from one stream, and at
most about half a point on the synthetic streams, where \mice{} itself gains 6.3 to 14.5 points over the window. The
synthetic streams are pure real drift with a fixed input distribution, cleaner than real streams. The mechanism test
uses one backbone seed (with three independent data seeds), and the development backbone seeds share data.
Changing the number of concepts changes both recurrence frequency and return interval, so this experiment does
not isolate their individual effects. Real-stream confirmation concerns 300-batch prefixes from four sources.
The safeguard is against the window, not against \mice{}; the small observed losses against \mice{} satisfy
an empirical tolerance criterion, not a guarantee of non-decreasing accuracy.
Selection costs extra TFM calls per batch (30\% more calls and about twice the time per batch in our held-out
run). On held-out real streams the gain over \mice{} is $+0.05$ to $+0.08$ points on average and slightly negative on
one source (Covertype).

\section{Conclusion}

Relevance selection gives an in-context learner the right past rows when concepts recur and the wrong ones when they
do not. As an expert of a mixture with a window safeguard, the first property is kept and the second is contained:
on these controlled streams the gain is larger for frequent recurrence and harder concepts, and is approximately
zero on average without recurrence. Individual losses remain within the pre-specified tolerance.
On 22 held-out real-stream prefixes this holds on every backbone seed: no measurable harm and a small gain.
